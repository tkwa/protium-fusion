# ✨ Warm rotor idle-power evidence

A 700 m/s carbon-rich rotor is a structural design problem that can be investigated directly. The more discriminating current requirement is **about 4 W of idle electricity per square metre of pump face**: at a face fraction of 0.245 this gives 0.98 W/m² of receiver. Existing small turbomolecular pumps do not demonstrate that areal idle load, and the retrieved hot-bearing experiment stops below 1000 K. These comparisons identify the bearing/motor and module-size work needed; they do not establish a fundamental loss floor.

The requested primary search was bounded to two full papers. Their relevant pages were visually checked. Raw PDFs are retained under `output/catcher-followup/warm-mechanical-cleanup/rotor/`, with hashes in [results.json](results.json).

| Primary experiment | Direct evidence | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Mazzocchetti et al., ISMB4, 1994](https://www.magneticbearings.org/publication/detail/ismb4_82/) | A 10 kg, 260 mm-diameter rotor operated at 27,000 rpm and 367 m/s. High-vacuum input was 13 W in suspension electronics/electromagnets plus 35 W at the motor, totaling 48 W; the summary table rounds this to 50 W. | The measured 48 W is about 904 W/m² of rotor disk projection. Replicating that complete small-module architecture would exceed the 4 W/m² comparison by about 226 times. This was an ordinary-temperature pump. The separately reported spin-down loss excludes suspension-supply power and must not be substituted for total input. |
| [Montague et al., NASA/TM-2003-212183](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20030039177) | Magnetic force and electrical power were measured up to 538°C (811 K), with rotating tests up to 15,000 rpm. A C-core required roughly 340 W to produce 190 lbf, approximately 845 N. The abstract's 8.45 kN conversion is a factor-ten typo. | This was an open-loop actuator test with mechanically supported rotor, rather than a freely levitated hot pump. Temperature-dependent resistance and growth of the magnetic gap increased power. The source establishes relevant hot hardware, but does not qualify 1000 K continuous service or the proposed areal idle load. |

The NASA thermal model found approximately 0.620→0.792 mm gap growth during nonuniform heating. Its rotor remained substantially cooler than the stator. Matching expansion and allowing the warm pumping stages to approach one another's temperature is therefore a concrete design variable. Suspending the rotor from cooler bearings and placing drive electronics behind thermal isolation is another route, with the actual shaft, field and radiation paths charged explicitly.

For comparison, holding a **48 W per-module loss** fixed would require a 12 m² pump face to reach 4 W/m², equivalent to a circular radius of 1.95 m. At 700 m/s the rim would rotate at about 3420 rpm. This is a scaling target only: bearing forces, rotor dynamics, clearance and electrical losses change with size. It makes larger, slower modules worth analyzing rather than multiplying a small commercial pump specification over the entire wall. In microgravity the steady external load also differs from the terrestrial tests; imbalance and transient loads remain.

At density 1800 kg/m³ and 700 m/s, the thin-ring stress scale `rho v²` is 0.882 GPa. An ideal uniform isotropic disk with Poisson ratio 0.2 has central stress `(3+nu)rho v²/8 = 0.3528 GPa`. Blade roots, directional composite strength, interlaminar load transfer and thermal gradients require the actual geometry. High hoop-fiber strength by itself does not qualify a slotted or bladed rotor. The main design does not require 2000 m/s.

Net radiation between equally hot stages vanishes in equilibrium. A cooled bearing or motor exposed to the 1000 K stage has a different load: a black 1000→300 K view transmits 56.244 kW/m² of viewed area. Its contribution per pump face is that coefficient multiplied by the effective emissivity, view factor and cooled-area ratio. Thermal shields and a small view can reduce it; the calculation does not assign a free cold bearing. This heat bypass must be accounted separately from bearing electricity. Conductive shaft/support leakage similarly follows the actual `k A/L` and temperature difference.

The next calculation should therefore specify a module radius, rotor and bearing mass, balance/clearance tolerances, magnetic geometry, coil resistance and controller loss, then close idle electricity and hot-to-cold transfer per face. Efficient molecular manufacture can provide the proposed material and precise geometry under the user's premise. It does not by itself assign motor or magnetic-loss coefficients. No additional physical test, new temperature limit or arbitrary safety factor is imposed by this screen.
